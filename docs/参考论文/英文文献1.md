Decarbonization involves a large-scale expansion of low-carbon generators such as wind and solar and the
electrification of heating and transport. Both space heating and battery-electric cars have significant embedded
f
lexibility potential. Granular price signals that convey abundance or scarcity of electricity are a precondition for
customers or aggregators acting on their behalf to exploit this flexibility. However, unmitigated real-time prices
expose customers to electricity price risks. To tackle the dual need of providing flexibility incentives while
protecting customers from cost shocks, real-time tariffs with a hedging component can be a solution. In such
contracts customers pre-agree an amount of energy and a consumption profile, while hourly deviations are
charged at spot prices. In this paper we analyze design options by using a dataset of anonymized smart meter
data and show that profile tariffs can bring electricity bill volatility to similarly low levels as fixed tariffs while
providing full flexibility incentives from spot prices.
1. Introduction
The decarbonization of electricity systems requires the integration of
large amounts of intermittent renewable generation. To achieve this
efficiently, demand response from flexible loads, including small-scale
assets that are regularly owned by residential customers, such as
electric vehicles, heat pumps, and batteries will play an important role.
These devices offer much more load-shifting potential than traditional
power-consuming devicesbecause theyare typicallyable to store energy
in thermal or electrochemical form. The electrification of the transport
and heat sectors mean that these devices will become much more
prevalent, implying a large flexibility potential for power systems.
In European electricity markets, residential retail contracts with
f
ixed tariffs (i.e., time-constant prices) are still widely used (ACER,
2023). As a result, grid-connected flexible loads of residential customers
have little incentives to shift or reduce their load in response to the
system’s needs reflected in real-time electricity prices. To unlock the
usage of flexibility there is thus a growing need to reform retail contracts
by introducing incentives for demand response, such as dynamic prices.
In this context, many economists have argued for the introduction of
real-time pricing, a form of retail pricing that passes day-ahead
wholesale prices directly to end users on an hourly basis, adding
network charges and taxes. However, such contracts fully expose

2. Literature
Demand response programs (DRP) can be categorized into price
based and incentive-based programs based on their operating mecha
nisms (Jordehi, 2019; Ludwig and Winzer, 2022; Parrish et al., 2020).
Price-based DR programs encourage customers to change their con
sumption behavior by exposing them to time-varying electricity prices.
Examples of these types of programs include Time-of-use (TOU) pricing,
in which customers pay different rates depending on when they
consume electricity; Critical Peak Pricing (CPP), where higher prices are
applied to a limited number of peak demand periods; and Real-time
Pricing (RTP), in which prices vary in real-time based on electricity
production costs and grid conditions (Jordehi, 2019; Ludwig and
Winzer, 2022; Parrish et al., 2020).
Incentive-based DR programs consist of lump-sum payments or rate
rebates to incentivize customers to shift their load or allow third parties
to control their electricity use during critical periods. Specific examples
of these programs include Direct Load Control (DLC), in which utilities
directly and remotely control specific devices; Load Curtailment, in
which customers voluntarily reduce their energy use during peak pe
riods; Demand Response, where customers bid to reduce their load in
exchange for a financial reward; and Critical Peak Rebate programs
(CPR), in which customers receive rebates for reducing load (Jordehi,
2019; Ludwig and Winzer, 2022; Parrish et al., 2020).
Meta-analyses studying the impact of DRPs consistently find that the
amountofloadshiftingincreases as the price ratio betweenpeak andoff
peak prices increases and as customers are equipped with enabling
technologies such as programmable thermostats or other forms of
automated load control (Faruqui et al., 2017; Faruqui and Sergici, 2010;
Parrish et al., 2020; Stromback et al., 2011).
Recent events, such as the European and Texas energy crises of 2022
and 2021, illustrate that in addition to short-term fluctuations in
renewable energy supply, political risk and extreme weather events can
lead to significant energy shortages over longer periods of time, ranging
from days to months. In these situations, traditional DRPs can fall short
because, while they provide clear incentives for short-term load shifting,
most do not incentivize load reduction during sustained periods of
scarcity. Additional energy conservation incentives are needed to
address persistent shortages. These could be provided either by exposing
customers to variable wholesale prices (as in the case of RTP contracts),
or through load reduction payments that reward customers for their
energy savings relative to a consumption baseline.
According to a study by the European Union’s Agency for the
Cooperation of Energy Regulators (ACER), RTP retail contracts were
offered in only one-third of EU member states (8 out of 24) in 2022,
while fixed-price contracts were still offered in the vast majority of
countries (21 out of 24) (ACER, 2023). Moreover, in countries where
these contracts are available (e.g., Norway, a non-EU member), a sig
nificant share of variable price contracts provided “little or no hedge
against price risks" (Willems et al., 2022).
A number of studies on the uptake of variable price contracts
consistently show that customers have a strong preference for contracts
with lower price risk (Dütschke and Paetz, 2013; Dutta and Mitra, 2017;
Kessels et al., 2016; M. Nicolson et al., 2017; M. L. Nicolson et al., 2018;
Stenner et al., 2015). This is further underscored by the evolution of past
energy crises, which highlight the large value that customers and poli
cymakers place on price continuity. For example, between September
2021 and January 2023, governments in several European countries
spent between 2 and 7 percent of GDP on ad hoc measures to protect
households and industry from high energy prices (Schittekatte and
Batlle, 2023). While a minimum level of protection against excessive
prices could be achieved through concepts such as affordability options
(Schittekatte and Batlle, 2023), stabilizing customer bills will likely
require other measures, including the two-sided profile contracts we
explore in this paper.
Although increasing levels of automation (Fell et al., 2015) and bill
guarantees (Ludwig and Winzer, 2022) may reduce aversion to price
risk, customers and policymakers are likely to continue to have a strong
preference for contracts that provide efficient incentives without
increasing the volatility of electricity bills. As customers have been
shown to respond to average prices (Ito, 2014), variations in annual
average prices are likely to be of greater concern than monthly
f
luctuations.
To mitigate the effect of customer aversion to price volatility, load
reduction rewards can be used in combination with constant retail rates.
Their implementation requires the determination of a consumption
baseline for the consumer, which will be the benchmarktowhich energy
savings are measured. In addition, a suitable compensation for the
customer must be agreed upon. The most common methods for calcu
lating the baseline are the so-called day-matching approaches (e.g.,
HighXofY, MidXofY, LowXofY), exponential moving averages and re
gressions. All of these approaches are based on historical consumption
with or without subsequent adjustments, like weather patterns or pre
event consumption (Valentini et al., 2022).
A limitation of these approaches is that administrative customer
baselines based on the same customer’s past consumption values create
incentives for baseline inflation (Chao, 2011). This effect has been
quantified in various subsequent studies (Mohajeryami et al., 2017;
Wang and Tang, 2022). Baseline-inflation is particularly problematic
because it tends to increase the scarcity that DRPs were designed to
prevent. The incentives for baseline-inflation can be avoided if each
customer’s baseline is determined independently of their own con
sumption, for example by defining the baseline as a share of aggregated
load (Chao and DePillis, 2013), relying on different types of control
groups (Lee et al., 2019), or if customers are required to purchase the
baseline (i.e., pay a constant retail rate for the baseline amount) and sell
deviations from it as demand response (Bushnell et al., 2009; Chao,
2011; Hogan, 2009).
Profile contracts, which have recently been proposed by (Borenstein,
2021; Wolak and Hardman, 2022) follow the same approach of buying
the baseline to avoid incentives for baseline manipulation. In the same
way, as financial CfDs for renewable generators can de-risk investment
without distorting the dispatch (Schlecht et al., 2024), profile contracts
which do not depend on a customer’s own consumption can de-risk
electricity bills without distorting the load-shifting and energy saving
incentives of the customers.
Unlike incentive-based DRPs, profile contract baselines do not need
to forecast each customer’s counterfactual load (i.e., the hypothetical
demand profile without demand response) and can therefore be set
independently of each customer’s historical consumption, effectively
eliminating the incentive for baseline inflation. However, the possibility
of paying more than the fixed retail price could make profile contracts
less acceptable than incentive-based DRPs, as the latter do not have any
upside price risk. It follows that an important consideration in the
acceptability of profile contracts is their effectiveness in protecting
customers from price risk.
In this paper, we analyze different options for the design of profile
contracts and quantify the effectiveness of different shaped baselines in
mitigating price risk and reducing the volatility of annual average prices
faced by customers.

3.5. Choice of reference price
The deviation between a customer’s hedge volume and their actual
consumption can either be settled at the day-ahead market price, or
prices closer to real-time (e.g., intraday market, intraday auctions, sec
ondary or tertiary balancing energy markets or imbalance prices). In
general, as illustrated in Fig. 4, prices closer to real-time will reduce the
price stability for customers because they are more volatile, while at the
same time improve the efficiency of dispatch as they incentivize the
system’s reaction to unforeseen events (e.g., generation outages) that
were not considered in the day-ahead price formation process.
If deviations are settled at the day-ahead market price, the cost
impact of consumption decisions is perfectly known in advance, making
it easier for customers to react to prices and schedule their consumption
accordingly. However, customers do not have an incentive to react to
subsequent price changes in the intraday or balancing energy market
when their load is charged at the day-ahead price.
Alternatively, deviations from the hedge profile can be settled at a
price closer to real-time. The main advantage of this approach is that

4.1. Scenarios
In our quantitative assessment, we compare the impact of the of the

Scenario 1 assumes full exposure to the day-ahead spot price

6. Discussion and limitations
The results we present highlight the potential of profile tariffs to
bridge the gap between real-time tariffs on the one hand and fixed tariffs
on the other hand. The results also show that the introduction of a
hedging componentin real-time tariff already significantly improves bill
stability, regardless of the precise implementation chosen. Unless future
analyses reveal a significant benefit of more sophisticated profile shapes,
it would therefore seem sensible to start with a simple baseload hedge,
for which there is a liquid market to reduce transaction cost and avoid
10 
C. Winzer et al.
Energy Policy 195 (2024) 114358 
unnecessary risk premia for more complex products.
In addition to their risk-reducing impact, hedge contracts would also
improve customer incentives both for load-shifting and for load reduc
tion. Customers who react to market prices by consuming less during
high-price years or shifting load from high-price periods to low-price
periods will benefit from lower average prices than their peers. By
contrast to fixed price contracts, where incentives for load-shifting and
load reduction are quickly diluted, when the prices are averaged across a
larger group of customers, profile contracts retain load-shifting and
energy saving incentives even if prices are averaged across larger
customer groups.
The magnitude of benefits for flexible customers which are enabled
by profile contracts will increase as the flexibility of the demand-side
increases. We therefore expect that the increasing penetration of new
f
lexible loads such as electric vehicles and heat-pumps will lead to an
increasing demand for profile contracts.
In addition to the benefits which theyprovideto the customers,retail
profile contracts could also reduce the risk exposure of retail companies.
By contrast to today’s unlimited volume, fixed price contracts, which
depending on their hedging strategy– may leave retail companies partly
unhedged during simultaneous high-price, high-demand years, profile
contracts transfer this risk to the consumers. This will reduce the risk of
retail companies, and could lead to welfare increases, because it allows
risk-averse customers increase bill protection by overhedging, while
providing incentives for flexible customers to reduce their demand in
response to high prices.
Our study is subject to several limitations that highlight need for
further research.
First, the customers in our simulation are not actually subject to the
tariffs we test. Our estimates of price reaction in the form of load-shifting
are therefore only illustrative. It would be beneficial to take actual price
reactions, both in form of load-shifting and load reduction, into account
in future analyses to quantify the benefit of profile tariffs more precisely.
Second, we have not calculated the impact of hedge volume scaling
ontheaverageprices ofhedgecontracts. Even if wedid, the limitation of
our dataset to two years would not allow us to make finalconclusions on
whether scaling of hedge volumes ex-post is an important feature to
protect customers or whether it is not necessary.
Third, in absence of data regarding customer demographics, we
cannot draw conclusions about the distributional implications of the
tariff design. Analyzing distributional implications could be an inter
esting topic for future research.
Fourth, in absence of data regarding device ownership, we had to use
a simplified algorithm for grouping the customers based on their load
profile shapes. Improvements of the grouping algorithm could poten
tially improve the results of more specific profiles. Such groupings could
be based on device ownership or the consumption profile.
Fifth, while we have quantified potential impacts and benefits for
retail customers, profile contracts are less simple and straightforward as
f
ixed price contracts. Further research would therefore be helpful to
assess the acceptance of profile contracts, and verify, to what extent
acceptance could be increased by changes to contract design- such as
bill garantees or price guarantees– or different communication
approaches.
7. Conclusion and policy implications
Retail hedging contracts could protect customers from price-surges
caused by technical failures, extreme weather events or geopolitical
supply cuts for long-enough until supply capacity is increased. At the
same time, retail hedging contracts would expose customers to the full
incentives of real-time prices, enabling them to lower their bill by
reducing shortages through temporary load-shifting or load-reduction
measures.
This is also an important insight for efforts to reform electricity
markets. As a response to the crisis, the European Commission has
proposed a reform of EU electricity market, including a provision to
mandate retailers to offer fixed-price fixed-term retail contracts. Our
results show that such mandates could be improved by promoting pro
f
ile tariffs rather than fixed tariffs that hamper flexibility use.
Within this paper, we discussed the optimal design of such contracts.
Weshowthat already a simple baseload hedge significantly reduces bill
volatility. While more fine-grained choices of hedge profile and scaling
can improve the fit of the hedge, in our limited observation horizon of
two years without a pronounced cold period, we cannot show the effect
of hedge scaling during cold spells, for which it would be expected to be
particularly helpful.
Further analyses will be needed to empirically quantify the expected
load-shifting and load-reduction potential for different contract designs,
customer groups and shortage scenarios and quantify the impact on
customer bills for a longer range of observation years. Other open topics
include suitable hedging strategies and products for retail companies,
the assessment of distributional implications or potential externalities
caused by insufficient customer hedging, and the assessment of
customer acceptance.
